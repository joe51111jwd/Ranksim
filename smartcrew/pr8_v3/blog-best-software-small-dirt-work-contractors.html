Dirt work contractor software for small dirtwork crews

Andrew Drish, founder
Published October 25, 2026. Updated October 29, 2026

Dirt work contractor software for a small crew has to be simple enough that your guys use it on day one. And it has to cover what actually leaks money: hours, machines, loads and job profit. For a crew of one to four quoting homeowners, a field service app like QuoteIQ or Contractor+ fits. For a dirtwork company with 5 to 100 employees, that's what we built SmartCrew for. Buy it in that order. Count who needs a login. Skip anything that needs a full-time person to run.

The short version

  • Small means two different things. One to four people quoting homeowners need a field service app. A dirtwork company with 5 to 100 employees, running its own iron and trucks, needs SmartCrew.
  • A 5 to 20 person dirtwork company needs four things first, in order: clean timecards, job cost against the bid, machines, and haul tickets.
  • Getting it wrong rarely shows up as one big loss. It's small leaks: minutes on timecards, machine hours that never hit a job, tickets that never get billed.
  • Seat math decides the real price at 12 guys: user caps, admin licenses and who counts as a paid user.
  • Some records aren't optional. OSHA asks for daily excavation inspections, and DOT asks for driver vehicle inspection reports on trucks it covers.
  • SmartCrew is built for dirtwork companies with 5 to 100 employees. Pricing depends on your size and features; you get a number on a short call with Andrew.

What does a small dirtwork company actually need from software?

Four things, in this order: timecards you can trust, job cost against the bid, your machines, and your loads. Daily reports, safety forms and scheduling come right after. Everything else can wait.

Small here means about 5 to 20 people. The owner still runs a machine some days. The office is one or two people, and a lot of the paperwork happens at night. You don't need more software. You need fewer places to look.

Why timecards first? Because every other number sits on top of them. If the hours are guesses written down on Friday, the job cost is a guess, the machine hours are a guess, and the next bid is built on guesses.

What does it cost a small company to get this wrong?

Not one big loss. A lot of small leaks you never see on a single bill. These are worked examples with round numbers, not statistics; put your own numbers in.

  • Timecards from memory. Take 12 guys whose time is off by 15 minutes a day. That's 12 × 0.25 × 5 = 15 hours a week. At $35 an hour with burden, it's $525 a week, or $21,000 over 40 working weeks.
  • Machine hours that never hit the job. An excavator carried in the bid at $90 an hour loses 2 hours a day to nobody writing it down. Over a 5-day week that's 2 × 5 × $90 = $900 of cost missing from the job report. The job looks better than it is, and you bid the next one off that.
  • Lost haul tickets. One truck loses one 14-yard ticket a week at $10 a yard. That's $140 a week you can't bill, $5,600 over 40 weeks.

The worst leak is the one every dirtwork owner knows: never really knowing how much money you're making on a job until it's too late.

Which records can't a small dirtwork company skip?

Two daily records come from federal rules, not from any software company. Whatever app you use, or none, they still apply.

Excavation inspections (OSHA). Under 29 CFR 1926.651(k), a competent person has to inspect excavations, the areas next to them and protective systems daily, before work starts and as needed during the shift, and again after every rainstorm or other hazard-increasing event, whenever workers could be exposed.

Driver vehicle inspection reports (DOT). Under 49 CFR 396.11, drivers of covered commercial vehicles prepare a written report at the end of each day's work on each vehicle, covering brakes, steering, lights, tires and more. No report is needed if no defect was found. Reports are kept for three months and may be electronic. The rule doesn't apply to a motor carrier operating only one commercial vehicle. Check with FMCSA or your state on which of your trucks are covered.

Software doesn't make you compliant. What it changes is whether the record exists and whether you can find it when someone asks. In SmartCrew, DOT and daily inspections, JSAs, toolbox talks and incident reports are done on the phone and kept with the machine or the job.

Why do small contractors get stuck between two kinds of software?

Because most software is built for somebody else. Time tracking apps are built for every trade, so they stop at the timecard. Enterprise software is built for big companies, so it comes with setup, screens and prices a 12-person outfit doesn't need.

That's the gap a small company falls into. You end up with a time app, a spreadsheet for job cost, a notebook in the shop, a folder of haul tickets and a group text. Each piece is cheap. Together they cost you an evening a week.

What about work order software for excavation?

"Work order" means two things on a dirt job. One is the job itself: where the crew goes today, with which machines, and what they're doing. The other is the shop: the repair ticket when a machine breaks.

Most work order apps are built for the first kind, for service businesses running lots of short customer visits. A dirtwork company needs both. In SmartCrew the schedule and dispatch send marching orders to the crew's phones, with the job and the equipment assigned. When an operator reports a problem from the cab, it becomes a service request in the shop queue, with who reported it, how urgent it is and where the repair stands.

Field service apps do the customer side well. QuoteIQ calls itself the all-in-one CRM built by home service contractors. Contractor+ says you can log equipment hours, fuel and maintenance on a project. Projul says its own equipment hours are not a replacement for a dedicated fleet management system. We couldn't find a shop queue for machine repairs on any of the three sites.

How should a small dirtwork owner choose?

Pick the software that fixes your most expensive week with the fewest moving parts.

  1. Write down your last bad week

    Was it timecards, a machine down, a lost ticket or a job that went sideways? That's what the software has to fix first.

  2. Count the logins

    Who clocks in, who runs a machine, who drives a truck, who approves time. That number drives the price more than the plan name does.

  3. Ask who sets it up

    At 12 guys you don't have an IT person. With SmartCrew our team sets up your account, migrates your information, organizes your workflows and trains your people.

  4. Hand the phone to your least techy guy

    Our crews aren't tech savvy. And they don't need to be. If he can't clock in and pick his machine without help, keep looking.

  5. Start with one thing

    Start with the features you need most and expand from there. Most companies are up and running within a few weeks.
