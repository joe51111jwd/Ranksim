# Ranking mechanics: what is documented, for the Ranksim theory

Research memo, 2026-11-19, from public web sources only. Nothing about this project, the client, or any person was sent to an outside service. Scope: mechanisms that have testable consequences in daily US/en top-10 data (Serper, three pulls a day). Companion memo on split-test edit effects: `evals/claude/research/edit-effects.md`.

Evidence labels. STRONG: primary documents (leaked API field definitions, sworn testimony, trial exhibits, court opinions, patent text for what it describes, peer-reviewed measurements, Google's own published numbers). MEDIUM: public remarks by Google staff, vendor studies that state data and method, consistent reports from several outlets. WEAK: one secondary source, analyst inference, unverified claims, folklore. Two standing caveats: a patent shows a design, not a deployment. A leaked field shows that a signal exists, not its weight or how it is used.

## 1. New entrants

### 1.1 How a page reaches the top 10 at all
- Pandu Nayak, sworn testimony, 18 Oct 2023 (Tr. 6331 to 6332): retrieval gets "of the order of tens of thousands of documents", lightweight scoring cuts that to "several hundred", then a "detailed ranking with your best scoring functions" brings it down to ten. https://thecapitolforum.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/20231018-APM-BT24-AM-Google.pdf [STRONG]
- Trial exhibit PXR0357 (notes of a 31 Jan 2025 call with Nayak): "Top-level signals are a linear combination of log of individual raw signals." Signals are "monotonic". Google uses "over 100 raw signals". Q* is "Google's measure of quality of a document". Navboost is "a measure of how frequently users (subset by location and device type) click on a particular document for a particular query ... Uses most recent 13 months of data" and is "a QD table, a query-to-document lookup table". "Twiddlers - re-rank a set of already selected results". https://www.justice.gov/atr/media/1398866/dl [STRONG; call notes, not testimony]

### 1.2 The handicap: a new URL has no click history
- Nayak (Tr. 6335): "clicks accrete over time, which means older pages, potentially stale pages, tend to have more clicks than fresh pages which may start out with no clicks at all ... you need to compensate for that in some way." Same transcript as above. [STRONG]
- Remedies opinion, 2 Sep 2025: Navboost is a "memorization system" and "just a giant table" (Lehman testimony), it "runs on 13 months of Google click-and-query data", "the equivalent of over 17.5 years of Bing data". Glue is a "super query log" holding the query, the 10 blue links and SERP features, clicks, hovers and duration (pp. 89 and 153). https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-dcd-1_20-cv-03010/pdf/USCOURTS-dcd-1_20-cv-03010-3.pdf [STRONG]
- Nayak (Tr. 6338 to 6339): a 2017 launch cut Navboost data "by about a third" with "no meaningful change in search quality". [STRONG]
- Leak: Navboost click records (QualityNavboostCrapsCrapsData) are sliced by country and device and carry patternLevel: "More general patterns get higher values. For URL patterns this field = 0." CompressedQualitySignals carries crapsNewUrlSignals, crapsNewHostSignals, crapsNewPatternSignals and crapsAbsoluteHostSignals ("Impressions, unsquashed, host level"). So click statistics exist at URL, URL-pattern and host level, with separate fields for new URLs and new hosts. https://google-api-content-warehouse.hexdocs.pm/0.4.0/GoogleApi.ContentWarehouse.V1.Model.QualityNavboostCrapsCrapsData.html and https://google-api-content-warehouse.hexdocs.pm/0.4.0/GoogleApi.ContentWarehouse.V1.Model.CompressedQualitySignals.html [STRONG that the fields exist; their use is undocumented]
- Patent US 8,898,152 (priority 2008): "new documents which may be highly pertinent as results for a given query can have low search result rankings". The claims score a result with selection data borrowed from a similar document, and the weighting can favour the document's own data as its own selections grow. https://patents.google.com/patent/US8898152 [STRONG as a design]
- Instant Glue: Resoneo says it uses Glue signals from only the last 24 hours, refreshed about every 10 minutes, for breaking queries. I could not open the article (HTTP 403) and could not find the term in the leak docs. https://www.resoneo.com/google-leak-part-5-click-data-navboost-glue-and-beyond-google-is-watching-you/ [WEAK]. Mike King cites a 2016 RealTimeBoost slide that lists "Instant Navboost (soon)". https://ipullrank.com/google-algo-leak [MEDIUM]

### 1.3 What a new URL carries from day one: site-level priors
- siteAuthority: "converted from quality_nsr.SiteAuthority, applied in Qstar" (CompressedQualitySignals, above). Site NSR fields include titlematchScore, siteQualityStddev ("spread of the page-level PQ ratings of a site"), site-level impressions and nsrEpoch. https://google-api-content-warehouse.hexdocs.pm/0.4.0/GoogleApi.ContentWarehouse.V1.Model.QualityNsrNsrData.html [STRONG]
- hostAge: "The earliest firstseen date of all pages in this host/domain. These data are used in twiddler to sandbox fresh spam in serving time." https://google-api-content-warehouse.hexdocs.pm/0.4.0/GoogleApi.ContentWarehouse.V1.Model.PerDocData.html [STRONG]. This is host age, not page age. It bears on new domains, not on new pages of old domains. Mueller (2021, below) says there is no sandbox that keeps new pages out.
- Patent US 7,346,839 (priority 2003; inventors include Acharya, Cutts, Dean and Haahr): a recent document "may be scored lower than an older document that has a larger number of links". The rate of new links counts. "a typical, 'legitimate' document attracts back links slowly", and a spike may "signal attempts to spam". https://patents.google.com/patent/US7346839 [STRONG as a design]

### 1.4 Freshness can offset the handicap, on some queries only
- Google: "We have various 'query deserves freshness' systems designed to show fresher content for queries where it would be expected." https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ranking-systems-guide [STRONG]
- Nayak (Tr. 6334): sports news wants pages from this morning, a laptop buyer wants "laptop reviews from 2023" not 2022, and for a turkey recipe "the recipe from ten years ago is actually better". [STRONG]
- Leak (PerDocData, above): datesInfo is "Used in FreshnessTwiddler". semanticDateInfo holds "meta data required by the freshness twiddlers". freshboxArticleScores holds freshness classifier scores. isHotdoc is "Set by the FreshDocs instant doc joiner". timeSensitivity is an "Encoded Document Time Sensitivity signal". [STRONG]
- Query deserves freshness was first described in the New York Times, 3 Jun 2007 (not opened, known through summaries). https://www.nytimes.com/2007/06/03/business/yourmoney/03google.html [MEDIUM]. A Nov 2011 freshness change was said to affect about 35% of searches. https://techcrunch.com/2011/11/03/googles-new-algorithm-update-impacts-35-of-searches (the Google post body did not load: https://search.googleblog.com/2011/11/giving-you-fresher-more-recent-search.html) [MEDIUM]

### 1.5 Vendor measurements of new pages (nobody publishes entry positions)
- Ahrefs (Patrick Stox, May 2025): of 1M URLs first seen in Sep 2023, 1.74% reached the top 10 within a year (6.11% in a filtered 2M English sample; 5.7% in the 2017 study). Of the pages that made it, 40.82% did so within 1 month. 72.9% of top-10 pages are over 3 years old, and the average #1 page is 5 years old. https://ahrefs.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-rank/ [MEDIUM]
- SE Ranking (May 2026, 100,000 SERPs, one snapshot): domains under 2 years old are 2.03% of top-10 and 1.42% of top-3 domains. https://seranking.com/blog/domain-age-ranking-factor/ [MEDIUM]
- Semrush (Mar 2023, 2,155 new domains tracked 13 months): about 41% had a top-10 keyword after 6 months. 27% of those that reached the top 10 stayed for all 13 months. Nearly 10% appeared in the top 10 for one month only. https://semrush.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-rank [MEDIUM]
- I searched for a published distribution of the first top-10 position of new entrants (Ahrefs, Semrush Sensor, Moz, Advanced Web Ranking, STAT, SISTRIX) and found none. Our own data is the best source for this.

### 1.6 Is there a honeymoon?
- Mueller (May 2018): with completely new content "the rankings ... would fluctuate quite a bit", for maybe "a week or two", adding "I'm just making up a number." https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-expect-extreme-ranking-fluctuations-publishing-new-content/251593/ [MEDIUM]
- Mueller (May 2021): "we don't have a lot of signals for that new content yet", "we have to make assumptions", "sometimes new content performs particularly well for a period of time and then it settles down again", "Sometimes things settle down a little bit lower, sometimes a little bit higher." He denies deliberate promotion or demotion of new content. https://www.searchenginejournal.com/mueller-mentions-google-sandbox-and-honeymoon-ranking-effects/408994/ [MEDIUM]
- Verdict: a deliberate honeymoon boost followed by decay is folklore [WEAK]. What is documented is estimation with missing click data plus freshness compensation. That predicts larger early variance and settling in either direction, not a systematic boost and decay. On non-fresh queries the documented bias runs against new URLs (no clicks, few links).

## 2. Same-day noise and day-to-day flips

### 2.1 Measured noise at the same moment
- Hannak et al. (WWW 2013, extended 2017): identical queries differ because of index updates, inconsistent datacenters, IP geolocation, A/B tests, and a Google "carry-over" effect from a query issued less than 10 minutes earlier. Two identical accounts querying in lock-step through one datacenter IP got average Jaccard 0.998 (same URLs) but Kendall tau 0.88 (order noisy). Change rates rise down the page. Rank 1 is the most stable. On personalized pages changes peak at 33% at rank 7, and non-personalized pages (the noise floor) show less than half as many at nearly every rank. https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.05011 [STRONG; data from 2012]
- Kliman-Silver et al. (IMC 2015): "Google Search results are noisy" for two browsers at the same time and place. Local queries are much noisier. Maps results cause about 25% of the noise. Location changes 18 to 34% of results for local queries, while politician and controversial queries stay near the noise floor. https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/ancsaaa/files/Geolocation-IMC.pdf [STRONG]
- Live experiments: in 2023 Google ran 16,871 live traffic experiments, 124,942 side-by-side experiments and 719,326 search quality tests, and made 4,781 launches. A live test usually starts at 0.1% of users. https://www.google.com/search/howsearchworks/how-search-works/rigorous-testing/ [STRONG]
- I found no recent published measurement of minutes-apart variance for US organic results.

### 2.2 Scraping-API artifacts
- Hannak: "search engine APIs sometimes return different results than the standard webpage." [STRONG, old]
- Google has required JavaScript on result pages since about 15 Jan 2025, which broke several trackers for days (https://searchengineland.com/google-disrupts-seo-tools-450872, not opened) and dropped the num=100 parameter around 14 Sep 2025 (https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-modifies-search-results-parameter-affecting-seo-tools/556080/, not opened). [MEDIUM]. Serper does not document its egress location, datacenter routing or caching, so those effects are unknown.
- The leak hints at several serving tiers: scaledSelectionTierRank is "a language normalized score ranging from 0-32767 over the serving tier", and compressed signals are "included in Mustang and TeraGoogle". Whether tiers make pulls differ is undocumented. [WEAK]

### 2.3 How much day-to-day movement reverts
- SEOmonitor (May 2026; 309,279 UK and US keywords tracked daily for 41 days, 1 Aug to 10 Sep 2025, no confirmed update in the window): mean absolute daily move 0.22 positions for the top 3, 1.00 for positions 4 to 10, 2.41 for 11 to 20. Of 365,801 single-day drops of 10 or more positions, 42.4% recovered within 1 to 3 days, 28.5% within 4 to 31 days, and 29.0% did not recover. Of 37,008 drops from the top 10 to 11 or worse, 24.3% came back within 1 day, 14.9% in 2 to 3 days, 16.9% in 4 to 7 days, and 43.9% were still out after 7 days. https://www.seomonitor.com/learning-hub/dynamic-depth-crawling-research [MEDIUM, vendor]

### 2.4 The rank transition function patent (US 8,924,380) and a relative
- Ross Koningstein, priority 30 Jun 2005, granted 30 Dec 2014, listed as expired 30 Jun 2025. When "a ranking factor associated with the document" changes (edits to the page, links to it, linking pages), the engine can publish a "transition rank" between the old rank and the target rank. Shapes described: a damped slow rise (about 70 days to target in the example); an initially inverse response in which "the rank of a document may initially decrease in response to a positive change", "for a period of approximately 20 days", then settles near day 70; a delayed response ("it may take an unknown (possibly variable) amount of time to see positive (or expected) results"); and injected noise for suspicious documents. The system watches whether owners react to the fake movement and treats that as a spam signal. https://patents.google.com/patent/US8924380 [STRONG for the design; WEAK for deployment, I found no evidence it is used]
- Implication: if active, the first days after an edit can move the wrong way, and a correct model of edit direction would need a delay of weeks. It targets rank manipulation, so ordinary edits may never trigger it.
- US 8,874,555 (Kim and Lopatenko, priority 2009): when click-based "quality of result" statistics for a document and query change significantly between time periods, this "is taken as an indication that either the document, or the user intent behind the document, has changed", and older click data is discounted; "the factor can be selected to eliminate all data for the document and the query that was collected before the spike began." Shingle similarity checks that the document really changed. https://patents.google.com/patent/US8874555 [STRONG as a design]

## 3. Predictable short-term dynamics

- Mean reversion: the SEOmonitor recovery figures above are the only large published numbers I found. A large single-day drop reverts within 3 days about 42% of the time. [MEDIUM]
- Freshness decay: US 7,346,839 lets a query-based factor "lower a score for a document that appears to be stale" and treats a downward trend in new links as a sign a document "is stale". The leak's FreshnessTwiddler works at serving time on extracted dates. Decay should show only on freshness-sensitive queries. [STRONG design; effect size unknown]
- Core update rollouts: Mueller (31 Mar 2026): updates "sometimes they have to work step-by-step, rather than all at one time" and "It's also why they can take a while to be fully live." https://www.seroundtable.com/google-on-why-core-updates-take-weeks-41133.html [MEDIUM]. Gary Illyes (Search Central Live, Barcelona, 2 Oct 2026, as relayed by an attendee): "Core updates take 2-4 weeks to roll out", and recovery after a core update typically takes 3 to 6 months. https://weareroast.com/news/google-search-central-live-deep-dive-barcelona-day-3-recap/ [MEDIUM]. Google: there are unannounced "smaller core updates"; "some changes can take effect in a few days, but it could take several months for our systems to learn and confirm"; wait "at least a full week after a core update completes" before analysing. https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-updates [STRONG]
- Do a site's gains arrive at once or progressively across its queries? Google does not say. Practitioners report waves through the rollout and reversals inside it (see the Nov 2026 report in section 0). The leak gives one structural reason to expect steps shared across a site's queries: site NSR carries nsrEpoch ("The epoch from which this NSR value is coming from"), and CompressedQualitySignals copies site-level values into each document's serving record, so each document's copy changes when its record is rebuilt. [WEAK inference]
- Host crowding and diversity: "Our site diversity system works so that we generally won't show more than two web page listings from the same site", subdomains usually count with the root domain, and more are shown when relevant (ranking-systems guide, above) [STRONG]. Announced 6 Jun 2019 (https://searchengineland.com/google-search-update-aims-to-show-more-diverse-results-from-different-domain-names-317934, not opened). Moz measured average diversity rising only from 90.23% to 90.72% after it. https://www.seroundtable.com/moz-google-diversity-update-data-27764.html [MEDIUM]. Indenting of a second same-site result ended in 2023, and that result is now listed normally (https://searchengineland.com/google-search-officially-stops-indented-results-433210, not opened) [MEDIUM]. In 2007 Matt Cutts wrote that "Google will show up to two results from each hostname/subdomain of a domain name." https://www.mattcutts.com/blog/subdomains-and-subdirectories/ [MEDIUM]
- Twiddlers in general re-rank an already selected set (PXR0357). King describes them as functions that "run after the primary Ascorer search algorithm" and can impose category limits. https://ipullrank.com/google-algo-leak [MEDIUM]

## 4. What can respond within days to an on-page edit

Page-level fields, which should change when Google reprocesses the page. [STRONG that each field exists; refresh timing is inferred]
- Dates: bylineDate is "the explicitly set date on the page", syntacticDate is "an extracted date from the URL or in the title", semanticDate is "derived from the content of the page" (King's reading, link above). PerDocData's semanticDate is "estimated date of the content of a document based on the contents of the document".
- lastSignificantUpdate: "LastSignificantUpdate as UNIX timestamp in seconds", with a "source" field. https://google-api-content-warehouse.hexdocs.pm/0.4.0/GoogleApi.ContentWarehouse.V1.Model.QualityTimebasedLastSignificantUpdate.html
- contentEffort: "LLM-based effort estimation for article pages". The same record holds URL-level chard, tofu and vlq quality predictions. https://google-api-content-warehouse.hexdocs.pm/0.4.0/GoogleApi.ContentWarehouse.V1.Model.QualityNsrPQData.html
- pqData: "Encoded page-level PQ signals" (CompressedQualitySignals). OriginalContentScore: 7 bits, 0 to 127 (PerDocData). King says only pages with little content carry it.
- Text and title match to the query, computed from the indexed text. PXR0357: Google's traditional approach "was in the style of Okapi BM25". RankEmbed is "a dual encoder model that embeds both query and document", so a page's embedding should follow its new text while the model itself is retrained only periodically.
- King reports the docs say "we only keep the last 20 changes" of a URL [MEDIUM]. US 7,346,839 says scripts, comments, ads and boilerplate "may be given relatively little weight or even ignored" when measuring change, so only substantial edits should count as significant. [STRONG design]

Slow site-level or click-based signals that one page edit will not move quickly. [STRONG that each exists]
- siteAuthority. Site NSR and its epoch. titlematchScore is defined per site: "Titlematch score of the site, a signal that tells how well titles are matching user queries" (QualityNsrNsrData, above), so a page title edit does not move it directly. siteFocusScore: "how much a site is focused on one topic". siteRadius: "how far page_embeddings deviate from the site_embedding". https://google-api-content-warehouse.hexdocs.pm/0.4.0/GoogleApi.ContentWarehouse.V1.Model.QualityAuthorityTopicEmbeddingsVersionedItem.html. Also hostAge, Panda-type demotions and clutterScore ("Delta site-level signal").
- Click memory: Navboost over 13 months. RankEmbed is trained on part of 70 days of search logs plus rater scores (remedies opinion p. 154), and PXR0357 says "Google trained RankEmbed on a sample from a single month of search data".

Recrawl and reindex latency.
- Illyes, 2 Oct 2026 (relayed, no methodology given): discovery of a new URL about 20 hours typical; refresh of a known URL about 30 days typical, slowest "weeks to never"; indexing end to end about 1.5 hours; title update and snippet update 1 to 2 days typical, slowest several weeks to months. Recap above, and https://www.seroundtable.com/google-search-crawling-indexing-serving-timelines-42225.html [MEDIUM]
- Google's title-link page: Google "has to recrawl and reprocess the page to notice updates to these sources", which "may take a few days to a few weeks". https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link [STRONG]
- Crawl frequency is driven by quality and popularity signals (remedies opinion p. 138). A low-traffic B2B page should sit nearer the 30-day typical refresh than a popular page. I found no B2B-specific latency data. [STRONG mechanism; no numbers]

The SERP title as a reindex marker.
- Google (17 Sep 2021): "title elements are now used around 87% of the time, rather than around 80% before." https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2021/09/more-info-about-titles [STRONG]
- Zyppy (Q1 2022 data, 80,959 titles on 2,370 sites): 61.6% shown differently in some way; titles over 70 characters 99.9%; 51 to 60 characters 39 to 42%; pipes replaced 41.0%, dashes 19.7%. https://zyppy.com/seo/google-title-rewrites/ [MEDIUM]. Ahrefs found title tags matched the SERP title 66.6% of the time (seen in a search summary, not opened). https://ahrefs.com/blog/title-tags-study [WEAK]
- Title-link sources: the title element, the main visual title, H1 and other headings, og:title, anchor text on the page, text of links pointing to the page, WebSite structured data (title-link page above). [STRONG]
- Reading: a SERP title that switches to the new title tag or new H1 text is good evidence that the page was reprocessed. No switch is weak evidence of no reprocessing, because Google may keep a rewrite.

## 5. Learning to rank and click models

Public LTR sets measure agreement with human relevance labels, not with a live engine's order, so they bound our question but do not answer it. In Yahoo's 2010 challenge (set 1, 519 of 700 features; "The queries, urls and features descriptions were not disclosed"), the single text-match feature BM25F-SD scored test NDCG 0.732 and ERR 0.429, a GBDT on all features scored 0.790 and 0.462, and the winning entry reached ERR 0.4686 (Chapelle and Chang 2011, https://proceedings.mlr.press/v14/chapelle11a/chapelle11a.pdf) [STRONG]. One good text-match score carries most of the label signal and hundreds of other features add about 6 NDCG points. On MSLR-WEB30K (136 named features including BM25, LMIR, PageRank and click features), the features with the highest LambdaMART importance were PageRank, query-URL click count and LMIR, and the strongest interaction was URL click count with query-URL click count; a full LambdaMART reaches nDCG@10 near 0.52 against 0.47 to 0.50 for interpretable constrained models (Lucchese et al., ILMART, SIGIR 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00473) [STRONG]. Istella-S has 220 features; I found no importance breakdown with named features. With real logs, Agichtein, Brill and Dumais (SIGIR 2006; 3,000 queries, 12 million interactions) raised NDCG@1 from 0.518 for BM25F to 0.622 by adding behaviour features, and BM25F plus behaviour (MAP 0.222) beat a production ranker built on BM25F, link and hundreds of other features (MAP 0.215), yet only 46 to 49% of test queries had enough interactions (https://cs.fit.edu/~pkc/apweb/related/agichtein06sigir.pdf) [STRONG]. Clicks are position-biased: in Joachims et al. (SIGIR 2005) users still favoured link one when the top two were swapped, and reversing the top 10 moved the mean clicked rank from 2.66 to 4.03 and cut clicks per query from 0.80 to 0.64 (https://www.cs.cornell.edu/people/tj/publications/joachims_etal_05a.pdf) [STRONG]. The cascade model best explained position bias at early ranks (Craswell et al., WSDM 2008, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=156688), and the DBN model added post-click satisfaction (Chapelle and Zhang, WWW 2009, https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2009/proceedings/pdf/p1.pdf) [STRONG]. Google itself said an early click signal "was biased by link position" and that it "avoids simply 'predicting clicks'" (PXR0357) [STRONG]. Reading for Ranksim: among ten results that are all relevant, retrieval has already used up most of the text-match signal, and the remaining order is carried by popularity, links and click memory that we do not observe.

## Testable implications

Each item: mechanism; prediction in daily top-10 data with three pulls a day; target (A = new-entrant placement, B = pair-order flips, C = edit direction); evidence.

1. **Host prior for new URLs.** Mechanism: site-level quality (siteAuthority, NSR, Q*) and host- and pattern-level click statistics exist from day one, while URL-level clicks do not (1.2, 1.3). Prediction: a new entrant's first-day median position is predicted better by its host's best current position on that query, or failing that its host's median position across our 100 queries, than by page text; entrants from hosts seen nowhere in our data land at 8 to 10. Target: A. Evidence: STRONG for the fields, MEDIUM for the link to placement.
2. **Click starvation, then convergence.** Mechanism: clicks accrete; Google "has to make assumptions" (1.2, 1.6). Prediction: in its first 3 to 5 days a new entrant shows a larger spread across the three daily pulls and larger day-to-day moves than an established URL at the same position, and the spread shrinks with days in the top 10; final direction is not systematically upward; entrants that start in positions 1 to 5 fall more often than they rise. Target: A, B. Evidence: STRONG mechanism, MEDIUM shape.
3. **Freshness only where the query wants it.** Mechanism: freshness systems and FreshnessTwiddler on byline, URL/title and content dates (1.4). Prediction: on queries whose top 10 has many titles or URLs with a year, new or re-dated URLs enter higher and then decay over days to weeks; on evergreen product-category queries entry position shows no freshness lift. Target: A, C. Evidence: STRONG existence, WEAK for B2B effect size.
4. **Order noise sits at the bottom and between close neighbours.** Mechanism: datacenter and index inconsistency and live experiments; same-moment Kendall tau 0.88 with near-identical URL sets; change rates rise with rank (2.1). Prediction: disagreement among the three same-day pulls is lowest at positions 1 to 3 and highest at 7 to 10; a pair that disagrees within a day flips on the next day far more often than a pair that agrees in all three pulls; within-day disagreement is the best single predictor of tomorrow's flip. Target: B. Evidence: STRONG.
5. **One-pull anomalies revert.** Mechanism: live experiments start at about 0.1% of traffic and datacenter differences are per request (2.1). Prediction: an order seen in only one of three pulls is tomorrow's majority order much less often than an order seen in all three; the day's majority order predicts tomorrow better than the last pull of the day. Target: B. Evidence: MEDIUM.
6. **Large drops partly revert.** Mechanism: transient reshuffles (2.3). Prediction: outside update windows, roughly 40% of URLs that drop 3 or more places in the daily median return within 3 days; a revert prior helps for large single-day moves and not for 1-place moves. Target: B. Evidence: MEDIUM.
7. **The November 2026 core update window.** Mechanism: a staged rollout from 10 Nov, reportedly landing from 13 Nov (0, 3).
